SYPRES · Master database · Planning document

Mapping every psychedelic randomized trial

A living systematic map of every RCT that has administered a psychedelic drug — any drug, any population, healthy volunteers included — paired with an open, exportable, citable database.

Revised twice after review 26 Aug 2026 Pilot: 95 papers included 45 with an extraction 16 consensus-equivalent

Scope decisions from review. Dose, psychotherapy, per-arm N, route, primary-outcome designation, race/ethnicity, and risk-of-bias were judged out of scope for extraction. This revision reflects those decisions rather than re-arguing them: dependent figures are cut or reduced, and the gap list is now four items, not eight.

The argument

Not a meta-analysis — a description of the field's shape

The contribution here is structural. This literature has so far been reviewed drug-by-drug and indication-by-indication, which means no one has counted it whole. The paper is the argument; the database is the instrument; each new Covidence export updates both.

Four claims the data can support that existing reviews do not make:

Nobody has mapped the whole field

Existing reviews are drug × indication slices. Healthy-volunteer pharmacology — 38 of the 45 extracted studies in the pilot — is systematically excluded from them, and it is where most of the dosing, safety, and mechanism evidence actually lives.

Papers are not trials, and the gap is large

In the pilot, 40 linked papers reduce to 32 distinct trials; one trial contributes three papers. Every "per study" count in this field is inflated by an unknown factor. We can measure it — including for unregistered trials, via source-paper DOIs.

No two trials measure the same set of things

Among the 16 verified-complete extractions there are 15 distinct domain combinations, 14 of them used by exactly one study. Trials do not agree on which domains to measure at all — the empirical case for a core outcome set, and the finding most likely to change practice.

Reporting integrity is measurable, and mixed

Extracted studies from the 2000s are almost entirely unregistered; those from the 2020s almost entirely registered. Of 18 registry-enriched trials, three were registered retrospectively and only six have posted results.

5 · The outcome landscape

Ready
  • 5A — heatmap of outcome domain × drug, cell = share of trials assessing it.
  • 5B — co-occurrence plot of domain combinations, showing there is no standard battery.
  • 5C — distinct sub-categories recorded within each domain (pilot range: 1–8).

What this claims, precisely. We extract outcome domains and a controlled sub-category within each (Psychiatric: depression) — not instruments. So the claim is not "trials use a scattered set of scales", which would need instrument-level extraction and is out of scope. It is that trials do not agree on which domains to measure at all: 15 combinations across 16 verified studies, 14 unique to a single study. That is the right level anyway — COMET-style core-outcome-set development establishes core domains first and picks instruments as a separate second stage. This figure supports stage one and says nothing about stage two.

6 · Statistical scale

Attrition contingent

Distribution of sample size by decade, by phase, and by healthy-volunteer versus patient population. In the pilot: median 24, IQR 15–32, range 6–176. A blunt, honest figure — these trials are small.

Attrition panel — contingent. Only 10 of 45 extracted rows carry both N randomized and N analyzed. The real obstacle is not reviewer effort but ambiguity: older unregistered studies often report a single N with no statement about dropouts, so a blank means either "no dropouts" or "not reported". An explicit "not reported" option makes the blank meaningful — and turns the panel honest, since "attrition was undeterminable in X% of trials" is itself a publishable finding.

7 · Design and methodological rigour

Registered only

Comparator type, parallel versus crossover, blinding level, and whether blinding integrity was assessed. Comparator type comes from extraction; design model and masking come free from ClinicalTrials.gov (pilot: 10 parallel, 7 crossover, 1 single-group; masking recorded for all 18 enriched trials).

Blocked for unregistered trials — roughly half the corpus, and the older half, which the registry cannot describe at all. The one remaining extraction ask (gap 3) closes this.

8 · Who is studied, and where

Partial

Trial geography plus population composition. Countries come from the registry — the pilot is heavily concentrated in the United States and Switzerland — alongside the healthy-versus-patient split and the handful of single-sex studies.

Reduced after review. Race and ethnicity are not being extracted, so the representativeness panel is out. If age and % female are added (gap 4, optional) a demographic panel becomes possible; otherwise this is geography plus population composition only — and the paper should say plainly that demographic representativeness was outside the scope of this map, rather than implying the data were unavailable.

9 · The dose landscape

Cut — out of scope

Cut after review. Dose extraction was judged not worth the burden: it is heterogeneously reported (mg versus mg/kg), varies by route, and is especially awkward for PK dose-response designs.

The data corroborate that judgement. Even ClinicalTrials.gov carries dose inconsistently — of 18 enriched trials only four state an explicit mg or µg dose in their arm labels and three more give an ordinal level ("low dose", "high-dose psilocybin"); the other eleven give none. There is no free registry-side substitute.

What survives: restoring the microdosing flag recovers a microdose versus full-dose split. That is a coarse binary, not a dose landscape — no dose-response — but microdosing trials are a distinct literature with distinct effects, and being unable to separate them at all distorts Figures 3, 5 and 6, where a 15 µg and a 200 µg LSD study currently sit in the same cell.

10 · Registration and reporting integrity

Ready · 1 panel cut
  • 10A — registered versus unregistered by year. Ready.
  • 10B — prospective versus retrospective registration, computed from registry and start dates (pilot: 15 prospective, 3 retrospective). Ready.
  • 10C — results posted to the registry (pilot: 6 of 18). Ready.
  • 10D — outcome switching — cut.

The objection to 10D is correct and decisive. In a database that deliberately includes secondary reports, a paper reporting a secondary outcome is the normal case, not evidence of switching; detecting switching would need a flag for the primary report of each trial, which we do not have and are not adding. A second problem confirms the cut: registered primary outcomes are free-text prose ("Percentage Change of the BOLD Signal", "area under the concentration time curve in oxytocin level") that will not map cleanly onto fifteen coarse domains, so most apparent mismatches would be parsing failures dressed as findings.

11 · Trial integrity and safety reporting

Ready · new

A negative-space figure: the finding is not what these trials do, but what they never check. Fully supported by the current schema — no new columns needed.

Denominator. Every number below is computed over the 16 verified-complete rows only. Other reviewers' rows are legitimately mid-extraction and including them drags every rate downward. Sixteen is far too small to publish — this shows the figure is computable, nothing more.

  • 11A — claimed blinding versus verified blinding. The masking level the registry claims, against whether the paper actually assessed blinding integrity. Pilot: 3 of 6 enriched trials with a complete extracted paper verified their blind; the triple- and double-masked trials that didn't are the interesting cells. Across verified rows, 81% show no blinding-integrity check at all. In a field where functional unblinding is the central methodological criticism, that contrast is the whole argument.
  • 11B — safety reporting completeness by population and era. 81% record no safety domain at all; structured AE collection appears in 3 of 16.
  • 11C — what "safety" means when it is reported. Structured AE collection (3), cardiovascular/QTc (1). Abuse potential and HPPD — the psychedelic-specific harms — appear zero times in the verified rows. That absence is the most pointed thing in the figure.
  • 11D — expectancy. expectancy is already an option in the template and has never been recorded for a single study. A zero is a legitimate and striking result for a figure about trial integrity: the field's most-discussed confound, available to record, never recorded. Worth confirming it stays zero rather than not-yet-reached as extraction completes.

⚠ A caveat about this whole gap analysis. I inferred the extraction template from the values present in the export, so options that exist but have never been used are invisible to me — which is exactly how I wrongly called expectancy a missing field. Other items flagged as gaps may likewise already exist. Worth checking the list against the actual Covidence codebook before acting on any of it.

S1 · Reliability of the review process

Ready

Per-reviewer rows can be exported from Covidence after consensus as well as before, so nothing here is time-limited: the database uses the consensus export, and the independent extractions are pulled separately for agreement. That also means this supplement need not stop at extraction — agreement is measurable at all three stages of the review, which turns a defensive methods note into a genuine contribution. Very few reviews report reliability at more than one stage, and almost none report it for a living review that will repeat the process.

  • S1a — title/abstract screening. Two independent include/exclude decisions per record, across the ~4,770 screened. Report Cohen's κ with raw percentage agreement: screening is heavily prevalence-imbalanced (most records are excluded), and κ is deflated by exactly that imbalance, so κ alone will understate agreement. PABAK or a prevalence-adjusted figure alongside it is the honest presentation.
  • S1b — full-text review. κ on the include/exclude decision, plus a second and more informative statistic: agreement on the exclusion reason among records both reviewers excluded. Two reviewers can agree to exclude for different reasons, and that disagreement is invisible in a decision-only κ.
  • S1c — extraction. Per-field, because the fields are different kinds of measurement: κ for categorical fields (phase, population, comparator), set overlap (Jaccard) for the multi-select outcome domains, exact agreement or ICC for the numeric fields (N randomized, N analyzed).

What still has to be pulled. None of the five exports we hold today carries screening decisions: screen, select, included and excluded are bibliographic record lists with no reviewer column at all, and only the extraction export has Reviewer Name. So S1a and S1b need an additional Covidence export type — the per-reviewer screening decisions — not a re-parse of what is already in data/.

Do it on a frozen snapshot. Agreement computed mid-extraction is meaningless — a "disagreement" is usually one reviewer not having reached a field the other has. Compute each statistic over records both reviewers have actually completed.

S2 · Registry versus publication

Ready

Richer than "the registry disagrees with the paper". The pilot's five mismatches have three distinct causes, and none of them is an error.

Across the four trials where both numbers are held, registry "actual enrolment" matched N analysed three times and N randomised twice (one matched both). The registry figure is not reliably either quantity — which is the reportable finding: an enrolment discrepancy is not evidence of error until you establish what each source is counting.

This also qualifies Figure 4. Grouping by registration treats an umbrella registration as one trial even when it covers several distinct experiments — Bershad 2019 (four-session dose-ranging) and Bershad 2020 (two-session fMRI) are different experiments under one NCT. The ratio over-merges in umbrella cases while unregistered reports without a parent DOI stay under-merged. Report it as "papers per registration", not "papers per experiment", and state both directions of error.

The structural insight this changes

ClinicalTrials.gov already supplies design, masking, allocation, sponsor and industry status, country, planned enrolment, primary outcomes, registration date, and results-posted — at 100% field coverage for every enriched trial.

Extraction effort should therefore never be spent re-collecting those for registered trials. Gap 3 follows directly: ask the design questions only of unregistered studies, where the registry cannot answer them. That is roughly half the corpus, and the older half — and it closes most of Figure 7 for very little reviewer time.

Beyond the figures

Three analyses worth running

  • Trial-level versus paper-level counts for every headline statistic, reported as a pair. The gap between the two is a result.
  • Concentration — by senior-author group, not by centre. The original framing needed institutional affiliations, which the Covidence export does not carry; country comes only from the registry and only for registered trials, so it cannot do this job. Author names can: they are present for all 95 papers. Last-author concentration is computable today — 56 distinct last authors, with the top five accounting for 29% of all papers (Liechti 6, Vollenweider 6, Nutt 6, Borgwardt 5, de Wit 5). Registry lead sponsors add an organisational view for enriched trials: University Hospital Basel alone sponsors 6 of 18. Methods caveat to state: last author is a proxy for research group and will mis-handle name collisions and shared senior authorship — report it as a bibliometric description, not a count of laboratories.
  • Unpublished trials — feasible, but a separate piece of work. Our registry ids come from papers, so this cannot be answered from the database alone. It needs a query in the other direction: search ClinicalTrials.gov directly for completed interventional trials with a psychedelic intervention, then subtract the NCT ids that appear in our database. The remainder are candidate unpublished trials, and no extraction is involved. Real caveats: while screening is incomplete a "missing" trial may simply be unscreened, so this must wait until the review closes; trials in non-CT.gov registries are invisible to it; and a trial may be published somewhere we have not indexed. A genuinely new number for the field — but an added task, not a free one.

A fourth idea — outcome-domain coverage versus regulatory expectation — is cut. It was under-specified: it needs a normative standard for what a phase 3 programme must establish, which we would be asserting rather than measuring. The purely descriptive version (domains cross-tabulated by phase) is computable, but the pilot runs 23 phase 1 to one phase 2 and two phase 3, and phase is recorded only for registered trials — too thin to carry a figure. Worth revisiting only if phase coverage improves.

Gap 3, specified

What the design and blinding options should actually be

Derived from what ClinicalTrials.gov encodes across our 18 enriched trials, so the extracted values stay directly comparable with the registry for the trials that have one.

Design — nearly right, and one thing not to add

Parallel versus crossover covers 17 of our 18 enriched trials (10 parallel, 7 crossover), and crossover being that common is itself worth capturing. Factorial is worth having as an option given how many psychedelic studies add a pre-treatment or co-administered drug.

Do not offer "single-group". Our one single-group trial turned out to be a registration error — see below — and in a database restricted to randomised trials a genuine single-group design should not exist. If a reviewer reaches for it, the question is whether the study belongs in the review at all, which is a screening decision rather than an extraction option.

Proposed: parallel · crossover (including within-subject) · factorial · quasi-randomised · other · not reported.

The one "single-group" trial is a mislabelled crossover

NCT00823407 — Clinical Pharmacology of MDA (Baggott 2010, n=12) — registers allocation: RANDOMIZED together with interventionModel: SINGLE_GROUP, which contradicts itself: a single group has nothing to randomise between. The giveaway is in the arms. There is one arm group, labelled "MDA", and it lists two interventions — Drug: MDA and Drug: Placebo — both double-masked to participant and care provider. Every participant receives both. That is a crossover.

The cause is a registration habit rather than a one-off typo: some registrants put every intervention inside a single arm group. NCT01951508 has exactly the same shape — one arm holding methylphenidate, modafinil, MDMA and placebo — but is correctly labelled CROSSOVER. Same structure, two different labels.

So the reliable signal is structural, not the label: one arm group containing several drug interventions is a crossover, whatever the model field says. The build now checks for this and warns, which also caught NCT01951508's missing allocation field.

Blinding — the three-option list has a real problem

"Single / double / open-label" is the classic vocabulary, but it is not what ClinicalTrials.gov records, and the mismatch is not cosmetic. The registry counts how many parties are masked, then separately records which. Those two things come apart in our own data:

Read the last two rows together. Both are "single-blind", but in one the participant does not know what they took and in the other they do — only the rater is masked. For a psychedelic trial that is close to the most important distinction on the page, and the classic label erases it. "Double-blind" is no better: it means participant + investigator three times out of four here, and participant + care provider the fourth.

Record who was masked, and let the level fall out of it

Proposed: a multi-select — participant · care provider (therapist or session monitor) · investigator · outcomes assessor — plus two mutually exclusive values, nobody (open-label) and not reported.

It costs the same to fill as three radio buttons, it is what a methods section actually states, it is identical to the registry's own vocabulary so registered and unregistered trials stay comparable — and single/double/triple/quadruple can still be derived by counting, so the classic label is kept without the information loss.

The care provider role earns its place here specifically. In psychedelic-assisted therapy the session monitor is the person least likely to be genuinely blind, whatever the protocol claims — and the registry records them as masked in 10 of our 18 trials. Capturing the role explicitly is what lets Figure 11A ask whether that claim was ever tested.

Allocation is not worth a field. 17 of 18 are registered as randomised and the database is by definition RCTs only. The one exception worth a value is quasi-randomised (alternation, day of week), which does appear in mid-century trials — fold it into the design list rather than adding a column.

Done now: every registry id is at least linked

A non-NCT trial previously rendered with no link at all — the worst outcome, a dead end rather than a one-click lookup. The build now constructs verified URLs for ISRCTN, ANZCTR, Dutch OMON and DRKS, and falls back to the WHO ICTRP search portal for any other recognised id. One subtlety handled: Dutch cells list the ABR number first (NL70508.068.1; NL-OMON55178) but only the OMON id resolves.

If a second adapter is ever wanted, write it for the WHO dataset, not for ISRCTN. ISRCTN's API returns the WHO Trial Registration Data Set, so one parser would also read ICTRP bulk XML and any other registry exposing that format — including prospective-versus-retrospective registration and planned-versus-actual enrolment. Two differences from ClinicalTrials.gov to handle: study design arrives as semi-structured text rather than enums, and dates are DD/MM/YYYY. Not built yet, deliberately — the corpus contains zero ISRCTN trials today. A few hours' work when one appears.

Sequencing

What to do, in what order

  1. Now, before bulk extraction prisma_manual.json is filled and reconciles. Decide on gaps 1 and 2 — both very low cost — and add them to the Covidence template before the bulk of extraction runs, so they never require a retrospective pass over hundreds of studies.
  2. During extraction Run consensus, then switch the pipeline from a single reviewer's rows to the real consensus rows. Build Figures 2, 3, 4 and 5 on the growing set as a running check that the schema holds up.
  3. At completion Freeze a versioned database release with a Zenodo DOI, then write against the frozen version so every number in the paper is reproducible from a citable artefact.
